    "section_name": "Introduction Recently, Ni-MH and lithium iron batteries as high-energy vehicle power batteries have been developed rapidly because of their advantages such as high energy density, fast charge and discharge process, long cycle life, and non-pollution attributes. However, the battery capacity decreases after hundreds of charge–discharge cycles, which finally leads to the battery being scrapped. From the viewpoint of environmental protection, conservation of natural resources and lowering the cost, battery recycling is necessary. The authors summarize the methods of Ni-MH battery and lithium iron battery recycling in domestic and foreign research, including the hydrometallurgical processing method, the pyrometallurgical processing method and the combined processing method, and each method is evaluated. An overview is then given about secondary pollution, security problems and solutions in the existing methods, which lays a foundation for future recycling of traction batteries (Yu et al., 2014). A shortage of Li material is expected when electric vehicles become extensively used, and new battery technology is needed (Vikström et al., 2013). Minimum recycling rates of 25% by September 2012 and 45% by 2016 are required according to the EU Directive 2006/66/EC. The recycling rate will reach 50%–75%, depending on various types of the batteries (Miedema and Moll, 2013). The recycling processes of spent lithium iron phosphate batteries include thermal treatment, wet treatment, biological treatment and mechanical treatment (Al-Thyabat et al., 2013). Waste toner is dealt with by heat treatment (400°C, 600°C, and 800°C) to obtain carbon-coated ferric oxides, the structures, morphologies and electrochemical performances of which have been subsequently investigated as active materials for lithium iron batteries (Li et al., 2018). Hanisch et al. (2015) used thermal decomposition of the polyvinylidene fluoride binder to lessen the cohesion of coated active material particles and weaken the adhesion between coating and foil. Via incineration and impact liberation, 97.1% w/w of the electrode coating can be regained with aluminum impurities of only 0.1% w/w, 30 times purer than the comparative process. Fernandes et al. (2013) extracted and removed Zn(II), Fe(III), Co(II) and Ni(II) in the leachate with 2-ethylhexylphosphonic acid-2-ethylhexyl monoester (PC-88A). The lanthanide metal was extracted at pH = 1 and the recycling rate was more than 99.9%. Innocenzi and Vegliò (2012) pulverized the nickelhydrogen battery (<500μm) using 2 mol/L H2SO4 at 80 ^∘C for 3 h and 1 mol/L H2SO4 at 25 ^∘C for 1 h to obtain the leachate. The pH value was adjusted to 1.6 with NaOH and the rare earth metal was precipitated using sulfate precipitation. The resulting recycling rate was 99%, containing 64% lanthanide metal sulfate and 28% cerium sulfate. Nevertheless, the wet process requires a large amount of the strong acid, alkali, oxidant and extractant, and resulted in a large amount of waste liquid. Biological treatment with bio-acid instead of traditionally used sulfuric and nitric acids has less impact on the environment. Mishra et al. (2008) used the acidophilic Thiobacillus ferrooxidans to leach the metal in the cathode material of spent lithium iron batteries.",
