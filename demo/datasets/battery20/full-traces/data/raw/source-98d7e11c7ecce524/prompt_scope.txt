[E12fff82ce4] section=A combined recovery process of metals in spent lithium-ion batteries
Jinhui Li a,\*, Pixing Shi a, Zefeng Wang a, Yao Chen a, Chein-Chi Chang b

[E5ac917164d] section=ABSTRACT
This work proposes a new process of recovering Co from spent Li-ion batteries (LIBs) by a combination of crushing, ultrasonic washing, acid leaching and precipitation, in which ultrasonic washing was used for the first time as an alternative process to improve the recovery efficiency of Co and reduce energy consumption and pollution. Spent LIBs were crushed with a 12 mm aperture screen, and the undersize products were put into an ultrasonic washing container to separate electrode materials from their support substrate. The washed materials were filtered through a 2 mm aperture screen to get underflow products, namely recovered electrodes. Ninety two percent of the Co was transferred to the recovered electrodes where Co accounted for 28% of the mass and impurities, including Al, Fe, and Cu, accounted for 2%. The valuable materials left in 2–12 mm products, including Cu, Al, and Fe, were presented as thin sheets, and could be easily separated. The recovered electrodes were leached with 4.0 M HCl for 2.0 h, at 80 ^∘C, along with concurrent agitation. Ninety seven percent of the Li and 99% of the Co in recovered electrodes could be dissolved. The impurities could be removed at pH 4.5–6.0 with little loss of Co by chemical precipitation. This process is feasible for recycling spent LIBs in scale-up.

[E3d27a66785] section=1. Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are widely used in laptops, mobile phones, video-cameras and other portable electronics. The use of LIBs, which consist of crust, electrodes (usually copper foil for negative electrodes and aluminum foil for positive electrodes), electrolyte, and polymer (Contestabile et al., 2001; Lee and Rhee, 2003), has increased sharply in recent years because of their function update and cost decrease. It is necessary to recycle lithium cobalt oxide (LiCoO2)., which is adopted as cathode active material, because Li and Co are not only rare metals, but also hazardous to the environment. Some processes to reclaim Co, Li, Cu, and Al from spent LIBs have been reported, but so far, only a few of them have been applied in industrial practice (Bernardes et al., 2004; Espinosa et al., 2004; Xu et al., 2008).

[E32a1fb7157] section=1. Introduction
Pretreatment is necessary in order to recycle metals in spent LIBs. Shin et al. (2005) presented a mechanical separation process consisting of a two-step crushing, primary crushing with a sieve of 20 mm size and fine crushing with a sieve of 10 mm size, followed by a hydrometallurgical procedure for lithium and cobalt recovery. Lee and Rhee (2002) treated the LIBs in a muffle furnace, obtaining LiCoO2 by burning off the carbon, which was adopted as negative active material. Castillo et al. (2002) reported a recycling process containing thermal treatment, which aimed at eliminating the carbon and organic compounds in solid residues obtained from an acid-leaching process. Saeki et al. (2004) developed a process of co-grinding LiCoO2 with polyvinyl chloride, to form Li and Co-chlorides. Contestabile et al. (2001) presented a dissolution process, in which the battery rolls were treated with N-methylpyrrolidone to separate LiCoO2 from their support substrate. Acid leaching is an effective method to separate LiCoO2 from graphite and other materials. Several different acidic media have been tested to dissolve Li and Co in electrode materials (Zhang et al., 1998; Lee and Rhee, 2002; Nan et al., 2005; Xu et al., 2008). Alternatively, bio-leaching processes have also been tried (Mishra et al., 2008). Chemical treatment was usually used as a final step in these processes to recover metals in spent LIBs. Nan et al. (2006) and Mantuano et al. (2006) both presented a solvent extraction method to recover copper and cobalt, respectively. Chemical precipitation was used by Contestabile et al. (2001) and Castillo et al. (2002) to extract precious metals from the acid-leaching solution. Lupi et al. (2005), Freitas and Garcia (2007) both conducted electrochemical processes to recover cobalt from spent LIBs. In addition, Paulino et al. (2008) compared chemical precipitation and solvent extraction processes for the recycling of spent LIBs.

[E9e62e9ee24] section=1. Introduction
This work presented here aimed at testing a new combined process to recycle precious metals, especially Co. This process can be described as follows: (1) exploring new physical pretreatment methods to decrease both costs and pollution, (2) optimizing the condition of acid-leaching process to increase the leaching efficiency of Co, and (3) selecting appropriate precipitators and conditions to maximize the recovery efficiency and the purity of Co. By using a combination of ultrasonic washing, the investigation of acid leaching and chemical precipitation, a recycling process for spent LIBs has been developed, in which ultrasonic washing was used for the first time as an alternative process to improve the recovery efficiency of Co, and to reduce energy consumption and pollution.

[Ec32f558196] section=2.1. Materials
The spent LIBs used in this study were provided by TES-AMM Co. Ltd. LiCoO and carbon powders were fixed on Al and Cu foils as positive and negative electrodes, respectively. The components of LIBs were separated and measured in the laboratory as follows. First, the crust was removed with pincers and saws. Then, all of the parts were put into hydrochloric acid, heated and agitated at 90 -C for 4 h, dissolving all of the metals. The concentrations of different metals were measured with an inductive coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometer (ICP-AES). Finally, the content of each metal in the battery was calculated. The results indicated that Co accounted for 16% and Li, Cu, Al, Fe accounted for 2%, 10%, 3%, and 19% of the mass, respectively.

[E918947584e] section=2.2. Spent LIBs recycling process
The process for separating the materials in LIBs such as polymer, electrodes, crust materials, and electrodes is shown in Fig. 1. Spent LIBs were put into the crusher and the screen underflow materials were treated in an ultrasonic washing container with agitation, with an ultrasonic frequency and electric power of 40 Hz and 100 W, respectively. This process was aimed at separating LiCoO2 from the Al foils, and separating carbon power from the Cu foils. In this step, the influences of the size of the aperture, as well as of the temperature and duration of ultrasonic washing were explored. Then a 2 mm aperture screen was used to select the screen underflow materials called recovered electrodes from the washed materials mentioned above. Following this sifting, hydrochloric acid was used to dissolve the recovered electrodes, and the influences of duration, temperature, and H+ concentration on leaching efficiency were studied. Finally, a chemical precipitation method was used to remove the impurities in the solution and to recover Co and Li, in which the influence of pH was tested.

[Ee824c32f43] section=3.1. Crushing and ultrasonic washing
In order to ensure the efficiency of recovery, it is preferred that LiCoO2 should only be present in the larger products in minimal amounts. Using a crusher with a 2 mm aperture screen, however, left some Al, Cu and electrodes among the larger products. The result was the same when using a 4 mm aperture screen, and even when the 8 mm aperture screen was used, small amounts of electrode materials, such as Al and Cu still remained among the larger components. When using the 12 mm aperture screen, however, no electrode materials were found in the larger products (Fig. 2a). ICP-AES analysis results from the 12 mm aperture (Fig. 2b) indicated that Co accounted for a larger percentage of the underflow, while the impurities, such as Cu, Al, Fe, and etc., were present in a lower concentrations (Fig. 2c). As Co was combined into LiCoO2,, the content of Li changed along with Co. With smaller screen apertures, the amount of impurities entered into the undersize products of the crusher increased. A crushing process using a 12 mm aperture screen resulted in 28% Co and 2% impurities in the recovered electrodes, which represented the best results in our experiment.

[E8767bc915a] section=3.1. Crushing and ultrasonic washing
In order to determine whether ultrasonic washing and agitation are necessary for electrodes separation, experiments were conducted using each process separately, immersing the LIBs in a 55 ^∘C water bath for 10 min. During the manufacturing process, organic binder was used to paste LiCoO2 onto Al foils. After a longterm use, part of the organic binder had probably volatilized, and the residual part would not be as steady as before. Therefore, Li-CoO would not cling firmly to the Al foils (Fig. 3a), and it is easy to be separated by a physical process. When using agitation alone, most of the electrode materials still stuck to the foils (Fig. 3a ); when using ultrasonic washing alone, only part of the electrode materials could be removed (Fig. 3a ). But when the agitation and the ultrasonic washing were employed simultaneously, almost all electrode materials could be separated from Al foils (Fig. 3a3). It may be that the cavitation effect of the ultrasonic wave can generate greater pressure to destroy insoluble substances and scatter them in the water. The rinse effect of agitation then facilitates the process of separating electrode materials from Al foils.

[E066b4f0e16] section=3.1. Crushing and ultrasonic washing
Temperature also affected the processing efficiency. Using ultrasonic washing and agitation, 92% of the electrodes was removed at 55 ^∘C, but the removal ratio decreased to 76% at 85 ^∘C, and the electrodes could not be completely separated from the Al foils (Fig. 4a). This is mainly because at higher temperatures the force caused by the collapse of the ultrasonic cavitation bubble is relatively small, and it cannot completely destroy the binding agent between electrode materials and Al foils. Ultrasonic washing separation at a temperature below 55 ^∘C was proposed, both to conserve energy and to produce better results. In order to explore the relationship between removal efficiency and duration in ultrasonic washing, the electrode removal efficiency under room temperature was studied. Under room temperature, with 15 min of agitation, the same removal ratio as at 55 ^∘C with a 10 min agitation could be achieved (Fig. 4b). Thus, it is recommended that the duration be prolonged in order to reduce the temperature to room temperature.

[Ec015f9b4ef] section=3.1. Crushing and ultrasonic washing
A process of material separation was proposed based on the results of the above experiment. This process consisted of three steps: (1) spent LIBs were crushed with a 12 mm aperture screen; (2) the smaller materials from the 12 mm aperture were put into an ultrasonic washing machine with agitation equipment for 15 min; and (3) the washed materials were put into a container with a 2 mm aperture screen, which was determined by the equipment applied in this study and could be optimized in further research. Subsequently, the underflows, namely recovered electrodes, would be treated by an acid-leaching process in the next step. Through this process, three types of products could be obtained, as shown in Fig. 5. During the pretreatment process, only one step of crushing was applied and only water was used to wash the recovered electrodes. Compared with the two-step crushing (Shin et al., 2005) and with treating the LIBs in a muffle furnace (Lee and Rhee, 2002), this process is energy-saving because the energy used in ultrasonic washing machine is much lower than that used in crushing machine; compared with thermal treatment (Castillo et al., 2002) and treatment with organic solvent (Contestabile et al., 2001), it is more environmentally sound because little organic wastewater and toxic gas were generated.

[E4a6a317be0] section=3.2. Acid-leaching process
The efficiency of the acid-leaching process could be influenced by H+ concentration, temperature, reaction time and solid-to-liquid ratio (S/L). According to Zhang et al. (1998), the influence of S/L on leaching is not obvious. In order to optimize the operational conditions for the acid-leaching process, experiments were designed to explore the influences of reaction time, H+ concentration and temperature. When the temperature was 80 ^∘C (Fig. 6a), H+ concentration was 4 M (Fig. 6b) and reaction time was 2 h (Fi g. 6 c), a high leaching efficiency was achieved; 97% of the Li and 99% of the Co were dissolved.

[E6f049d6485] section=3.3. Chemical precipitation
After the recovered electrodes were dissolved by hydrochloric acid under the conditions of 4.0 M H+, 80 -C, 2.0 h, and agitation, the insoluble substances could be removed by a filter. The concentration of the main elements including Co, Li, Cu, Al and Fe in the acid leachate of recovered electrodes was 4.42, 0.76, 0.09, 0.19, and 0.38 g L ^-1, respectively. According to the differences of the solubility product constant between different M ^n+ (OH)n, these metals could be extracted step by step with the gradual addition of a 40% NaOH solution. For example, all Al, Fe, and Cu could be removed at a pH of 7.2, with almost no loss of Co in theory. Experiments were designed to study the change of metal concentrations in an acidleaching solution under different pH conditions. The results of chemical precipitation, calculated by testing different metal ion concentrations in solution, are shown in Fig. 7. They indicate that both the loss ratio of Co and the removal ratio of impurities increased with increasing of pH. In order to achieve a low loss ratio of Co and a high removal ratio of impurities (Fe, Cu, and Al), the pH has to be controlled in an approximate range of 4.5–6.0.

[E4953590139] section=3.4. Recycling efficiency and environmental impact
During the material separation process, 8% of the Co was lost; during the acid-leaching process, 1% Co was not dissolved; whereas purifying the Co-containing solutions with a gradual addition of NaOH would deposit 2–3% of the Co, along with other metals as well. In summary, about 89% of the Co in the spent LIBs could be recovered with the proposed process. It is a competitive, if not improved, result compared with some other reported processes, in which the Co recycling efficiency is about, or less than 90% (Nan et al., 2005; Dorella and Mansur, 2007).

[E37b36c8855] section=3.4. Recycling efficiency and environmental impact
The aforementioned recycling processes in introduction part are: using two or more steps of crushing, thermal treatment and solvent extraction, or combining two or more of the three processes. Commonly, the energy cost of crushing and thermal processes are high and the environmental impact of thermal treatment and solvent extraction processes are serious due to their potential to release toxic organic gas. It is clear that thermal treatment and solvent extraction are not good choices in practice, if there are alternative processes. The purpose of using ultrasonic washing was to reduce both energy consumption and pollution. Detailed information will be gathered in further studies. It must be remembered, too, that all existing technologies including the one represented in this paper are more effective in processing spent LIBs than in processing lithium polymer ion secondary batteries. However, recycling technologies for lithium polymer ion secondary batteries also need to be explored in time.
