    "section_name": "1. Introduction Challenges faced in WEEE (Waste of Electrical and Electronic Equipment) management are not only due to the growing quantities of generated waste, but also to their complexity. Numerous modern technologies are integrated in electrical and electronic products and each one of them is composed of various materials and components: WEEE represent a waste category characterized by high content of heavy metals and harmful substances that are hazardous for human health and the environment. They are produced in high quantities and this implies greater difficulties in the disposal processes; moreover, the extreme heterogeneity of the contained materials makes their recovery challenging (Sun et al., 2016). The principal items found in WEEE for economic importance are mobile phones (MPs) that every day become a part of the lifestyle of increasing numbers of people everywhere. This growth in popular use is encouraged by technological improvements and by more and better services from the equipment as they become more and more sophisticated each day and lead the users to discard their older models and buy newer, smaller, lighter or more modern handsets. There is an enormous growth in MP consumption worldwide which leads to generation of a large volume of MP waste every year (Sarath et al., 2015). It is estimated that in 2017 Europe 28, imported 210,784,723 MPs, for a total weight of 64,210 tons (elaborations from https://comtrade.un.org/data). MPs are subject to a continuous technological progress and to frequent replacement with more performing models, so their life time is rather reduced. The characterization of constituent components of the MPs is a crucial step in the disassembly procedure in order to quickly and accurately identify the components for which it may be convenient to attempt a recovery, separating them from the ones that must be stored or disposed of, each according to the more sustainable process. Materials contained in MPs are variable to some extent, based on the model and its manufacturer, with no fixed formula or list of contents applicable as such; for this reason, the list of materials in an average MP may also be misleading since different/several substances might be used as additives in very low quantities or traces by different manufacturers in the production of microelectronic components. Nevertheless, the general composition of MPs is identical in nature. Few are recent studies focusing on the fractional composition of MPs: Bhuie et al. (2004) generically cited their material composition but did not identify plastic materials; Tan et al. (2017) established that from manual dismantling it is possible to obtain plastic (25.75 wt%), batteries (21.12 wt%), printed circuit boards (PCBs) (17.67 wt%), metals (17.30 wt%), and displays (8.37 wt%). This product variability, both over time and from manufacturer to manufacturer, represents a significant issue when dealing with WEEE recycling: a treatment process would instead require constant inputs to ensure optimal performance in terms of efficiency and purity of the recovered materials. Also literature addressing the MPs plastic characterization is not exhaustive: this might be due to the fact that polymer recovery from mobile phones is still in its early stages and then not much interesting in economic terms. Kasper et al. (2011) identified polymeric components by the symbols or codes or both recorded on the surfaces and analyzed the plastic fraction recovered from PCBs by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). The Authors found out that the majority of the mobile phone frames and polymeric components were made of one of three types of polymers, acrylonitrile–butadienestyrene (ABS), polycarbonate (PC) and polyamide (PA). Palmieri et al. (2014) carried out a study to investigate the combined utilization of electronic and chemical imaging based techniques to characterize plastic materials from MPs, adopting plastic codes for initial identification of polymers and a combination of Raman spectroscopy, Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) and Hyper-Spectral Imaging (HIS) to characterize plastic waste.",
