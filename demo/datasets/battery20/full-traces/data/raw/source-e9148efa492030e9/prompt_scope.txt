[Ea7b5a1c047] section=Comprehensive evaluation on effective leaching of critical metals from spent lithium-ion batteries
Wenfang Gao a,b, Chenming Liu a, Hongbin Cao a,b, Xiaohong Zheng a, Xiao Lin a,b, Haijuan Wang c, Yi Zhang a,b, Zhi Sun a,b,⇑

[E85a39810c6] section=ABSTRACT
Recovery of metals from spent lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) has attracted worldwide attention because of issues from both environmental impacts and resource supply. Leaching, for instance using an acidic solution, is a critical step for effective recovery of metals from spent LIBs. To achieve both high leaching efficiency and selectivity of the targeted metals, improved understanding on the interactive features of the materials and leaching solutions is highly required. However, such understanding is still limited at least caused by the variation on physiochemical properties of different leaching solutions. In this research, a comprehensive investigation and evaluation on the leaching process using acidic solutions to recycle spent LIBs is carried out. Through analyzing two important parameters, i.e. leaching speed and recovery rate of the corresponding metals. the effects of hydrogen ion concentration, acid species and concentration on these two parameters were evaluated. It was found that a leachant with organic acids may leach Co and Li from the cathode scrap and leave Al foil as metallic form with high leaching selectivity, while that with inorganic acids typically leach all metals into the solution. Inconsistency between the leaching selectivity and efficiency during spent LIBs recycling is frequently noticed. In order to achieve an optimal status with both high leaching selectivity and efficiency (especially at high solid-to-liquid ratios), it is important to manipulate the average leaching speed and recovery rate of metals to optimize the leaching conditions. Subsequently, it is found that the leaching speed is significantly dependent on the hydrogen ion concentration and the capability of releasing hydrogen ions of the acidic leachant during leaching. With this research, it is expected to improve understanding on controlling the physiochemical properties of a leaching solution and to potentially design processes for spent LIBs recycling with high industrial viability.

[E97cc7ce234] section=1. Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are currently used as electric power sources in portable electronic products such as mobile phones, laptops, cameras (He et al., 2017) and electric vehicles because of their high energy density, long cycling life and low self-discharge rate (Zeng et al., 2015a). According to the perspective of global vehicle market, the sales of the electric battery vehicle is expected to increase from 5 million in 2015 to about 180 million in 2045 (Li et al., 2017). With a relatively short life span of about 3–8 years, a large number of spent LIBs have already been generated for about 200 million tons in 2017 and it will be nearly 400 million tons in 2020 (Gu et al., 2017). Spent LIBs have caught much attention because of the possibility of recovering valuable resources such as lithium, nickel, cobalt and aluminum and they are also considered as potential environmental contaminants due to the content of heavy metals and fluoride-bearing electrolyte (Gao et al., 2017). Regulations (in Asia countries, European Union, North America or Channel Islands) have defined the requirements to reduce the contaminants during recycling of spent LIBs (Joulie et al., 2017). The recycling of spent LIBs is of great importance for the environment and ensuring the safe supply of corresponding resources.

[E7337da872a] section=1. Introduction
Hydrometallurgy, pyrometallurgy and biometallurgy are three typical recycling technologies for spent LIBs recycling. In a pyrometallurgy process, plastic housings, conductive agents, binders and organic electrolytes are burnt off to facilitate the subsequent recovery of valuable metals (Nayaka et al., 2015). However, the disadvantages, such as high capital cost, emission of hazardous gas (HF), dusts and consumption of intensive energy, limit the application of pyrometallurgy (Joulie et al., 2017). Biometallurgy is a very promising technology for its low cost and modest apparatus requirement, but the poor adaptability and rigorous leaching conditions reduce its applicability (Liu et al., 2013). Therefore, with effective metal recovery and relatively low energy consumption, hydrometallurgy has been paid significant attention. In a typical hydrometallurgical recycling process, the leaching process with acidic solution is essential to effectively recover metals from cathode scrap, which is considered as the most important fraction of a LIB (because of the content of cathode materials and high purity aluminum foil) (Gaines, 2014). Before 2005, LiCoO2 was the main cathode material with 94% of the market share in 2015 (He et al., 2017). Although different cathode materials such as LiMnO2, LiFePO, LiNi0. 33 Co0. 33 Mn0. 33 O2 and LiNi0. 8 Co0. 15 Al0. 05 O2 are available in the market, their recovery are mainly focused on cobalt-containing spent LIBs (Gao et al., 2018; Lv et al., 2018) especially on LiCoO (Table S2). Only a few reports are available for the recycling of LiFePO4 using hydrometallurgy (Huang et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2016). The leaching of cobalt-containing cathode materials often use inorganic acids (e.g., H SO (Sa et al., 2015; Zou et al., 2013), HCl (Guo et al., 2015; Shuva and Kurny, 2013) and HNO3) or organic acids (e.g., citric acid (Yao et al., 2015), DL-malic acid (Li et al., 2010; Yao et al., 2016) and oxalic acid (Sun and Qiu, 2012)) as leachant. To increase the leaching efficiency, reductants (e.g., H2O2 (Gratz et al., 2014; Li et al., 2015), glucose (Chen et al., 2016) and NaHSO (Meshram et al., 2015)) are often introduced to reduce the metal ions from high valances (e.g., Co^3+, Mn4+) to lower valence ions (e. g., Co ^2+, Mn ^2+) which are more stable in the solution. Some natural substances can also act as leachant (e.g., citrus fruit juice (Pant and Dolker, 2017)) or reductant (e.g., phytolacca and tea waste (Chen et al., 2015)) to reduce environmental pollution and improve recycling efficiency.

[E611265960b] section=1. Introduction
Before investigating the leaching process, the understanding on the interactive features between the materials and solutions is the foundation to achieve both high leaching efficiency and selectivity of the targeted metals. For example, besides acting as the leachant, oxalic acid can precipitate Co (Zeng et al., 2015b), citric acid can present as a chelating reagent to re-synthesis cathode materials (Yao et al., 2015), and formic acid can selectively recover Al foil and reduce the addition of reductants (Gao et al., 2017). However, variations on the physiochemical properties of different acidic solutions are readily bringing difficulties to design the best leachant for spent LIBs recycling. At the same time, in previous studies, it is always necessary to explore the optimal conditions such as acid concentration, solid to liquid (S/L) ratio, reductant content and reaction temperature to achieve high leaching efficiency. The analysis and evaluation of different acid leaching conditions for spent LIBs recovery are seldom carried out, and to achieve both high leaching efficiency and selectivity of the targeted metals has been difficult. Three aspects, i.e. the interactive features of materials and acidic solutions, the variation on physiochemical properties of leaching solutions and the affection of leaching parameters, are therefore critical to be considered to have a systematic and comprehensive evaluation on effective acid leaching process during metal recovery from spent LIBs.

[E433701bd07] section=1. Introduction
In this study, a comprehensive investigation and evaluation on the leaching processes with acidic solutions for recovery of metals from spent LIBs (LiCoO2 was used for simplification) was carried out. Among different leaching parameters (e.g., acid species and concentrations, S/L ratios, reductant species and concentrations, leaching times, temperatures, leaching speed and recovery rate), the recovery rate is an important parameter to evaluate the viability of a leaching process, and the leaching speed is an important parameter to analyze the leaching kinetics or effectiveness of a leaching process. Through defining and analyzing these two parameters, the effects of hydrogen ion concentrations, acid species and concentrations on these two parameters were evaluated. In a typical leaching process, inconsistency between the leaching selectivity and efficiency was frequently noticed. Furthermore, the decisive factors of the leaching speed and recovery rate were identified by analyzing the leaching parameters. On one hand, 12 acidic solutions were used to understand the leaching behavior of cathode scrap from spent LIBs and to find out the correlation between the leaching speed and recovery rate of the corresponding metals. On the other hand, it is noticed that the conditions to ensure both high leaching efficiency and leaching selectivity can be achieved by optimized process design. Completion of these objectives will be expected to attain preliminary correlations to control the physiochemical properties of leaching solution and to potentially design process at both lab-scale and industrial levels.

Since the ability of releasing hydrogen ion for organic acids are lower than inorganic acids, the total hydrogen ion (including the released and unreleased hydrogen ions) in the leachants with organic acids are higher than that with inorganic acids at the same pH, leading to the recovery rates of organic acids higher than inorganic acids.

[E06281a7aad] section=3.4.2. Leaching with the addition of reductant
For the leaching processes of LiCoO in appearance of H2O2 under the same pH (Fig. 6), the leaching speeds and recovery rates with inorganic acids are lower than that with organic acids. Based on the higher ability of releasing hydrogen ion for the inorganic acids, the total reactive hydrogen ions in inorganic acidic solutions at the same pH are lower than those in organic acidic solutions, which lead to lower leaching speeds and recovery rates for inorganic acidic solutions. The acidic solutions, with a higher pKa1 usually achieve a higher recovery rate of metal values. Compared with the leaching processes without H2O2, the leaching speed and recovery rate of the same acid increases in a certain range after the addition of H2O2, indicating that the addition of the reductant promotes the leaching of Co and Li. But for ascorbic acid, the addition of the reductant to the ascorbic acidic solution shows a counteracting effect which is detrimental to the ascorbic acid leaching process of LiCoO2 (see Fig. 7.)

[E115c34af93] section=3.5. Discussion
Based on the above experiments and analysis, the preliminary correlations, which can control the physiochemical properties of a leaching solutions to tentatively design processes to achieve different purposes of the cathode scrap leaching processes, can be discussed as follows:

[E789cb8f353] section=3.5. Discussion
(a) To achieve high leaching selectivity and efficiency, the organic acids can be chosen as leachant. If the reductive organic acid, such as ascorbic acid, is chosen as the leaching acid, reductant is then not necessary.

[E4daf617e39] section=3.5. Discussion
(b) To leach the metals with high leaching efficiency and S/L ratio without considering selectivity, inorganic acids are better than organic acids for the recovery of cathode scrap from spent LIBs. HCl can leach the cathode scrap without the addition of reductants. Other inorganic acids can also leach the cathode scrap with the addition of reductant under the conditions as follows: S/L ratio of 100 g/L, acid concentration of 2–4 mol/L at 60 - 90 ^∘C for 60 min. Decomposition of H2O2 needs to be paid attention that the leaching temperature shall be well controlled. Although organic acids can help to decrease the decomposition rate of H2O2, its decomposition may be still severe due to the fluctuation of temperature and catalytic effects of the transition metal oxides. Other reductants (e.g., glucose (Chen et al., 2016), Ν _ d2 S2O3 (Zhang et al., 2015) and NaHSO (Pagnanelli et al., 2014)) can also be used in the high temperature leaching processes.

[Ee81b5a9f4c] section=3.5. Discussion
(c) For the industrial application, the high leaching selectivity, efficiency and high S/L ratio are important to decrease the cost of the spent LIBs recovery. For the organic acids, higher leaching selectivity and efficiency can be achieved but the treatment capacity is too low to be promoted. The inorganic acid leaching processes have higher leaching efficiency and S/L ratio, while the low selectivity has added the difficulty to the subsequent recovery steps. To solve this problem, the organic acid leaching process and inorganic acid leaching process can be combined to simplify the spent LIBs recovery process, and this strategy has been realized for industrial application in a local recycling company. As a pretreatment process, the organic acid leaching process can separate the Al foil from cathode materials and other metals are almost not leached. The recovery rate after the pretreatment process was shown in Table S4. The Al foil was only dissolved 10.09 wt% and the purity of Al foil was 99.94 wt% (Table S5, Fig. S5). Subsequently, the cathode materials can be leached out by inorganic acids with high S/L ratio and efficiency. The organic acid can be recirculated by distillation process. Therefore, the leaching process can reach a high industrial viability with proper process design.

[Eaf63214a9b] section=3.5. Discussion
In the organic acidic solution, hydrogen ion is sustainable released with pH > 1 and pKa1 < 1. However, the inorganic acid can leach all the metals out with pH < 1 and p Ka1 > 1. The reasons of leaching selectivity in organic acidic solution can be as follows: since the anion radius of organic acids is larger than inorganic acids, the ionization of organic acid is usually more difficult than inorganic acid, resulting in lower ionization capacities and lower H ^+ amounts for organic acidic solution. At the same time, the Al foil is usually formed by a thin layer of Al2O3,, which passives it, the lower amount of H+ in the organic acidic solution cannot react effectively with the Al foil in cathode scrap while the high mount of H+ in inorganic acidic solution can easily react with the passivation layer. Finally, covalent bonds (e.g., carboxyl groups, carbonyl groups, hydroxyl groups) in organic acids are much more complicated than inorganic acids which affect the properties of organic acids and result in different leaching efficiencies.

[E358b141c3a] section=3.6. Waste management of spent lithium-ion batteries
Spent LIBs are a significant waste stream which requires adequate management (Bahaloo-Horeh and Mousavi, 2017). The management of spent LIBs has been debated around the world, including international organizations, governments, academic, industry and non-governmental organizations.

[E042d631540] section=3.6. Waste management of spent lithium-ion batteries
Since it is both a local and global issue, several studies has been focused on the management of spent LIBs using the managerial tools such as life cycle assessment (LCA) (Liang et al., 2017), material flow analysis (MFA) (Sommer et al., 2015) and extended producer responsibility (RPR) (Bertuol et al., 2016). Zackrisson et al. (Zackrisson et al., 2010) optimize the design of lithium-ion batteries using LCA methods for plug-in hybrid electric vehicles. Speirs et al. (Speirs et al., 2014) evaluated the future of lithium availability for electric vehicle batteries under the MFA method.

[E99c9330c8c] section=3.6. Waste management of spent lithium-ion batteries
The life of LIBs, similar as electronic equipment, contains product life cycle (selling, storage at home, use, reuse, giving, export) and product end-of-life (recycling, landfilling, incineration) (Tanskanen, 2013). Recovery of the valuable metals is the preferred rout for the waste LIBs where landfilling and incineration should be avoided as far as possible for a sustainable environment and society. Strict global regulations have been developed on management of spent LIBs. For example, Japan Portable Rechargeable Battery Recycling Centre (JBRC) requires the relevant producers to collect and recycle rechargeable batteries (Terazono et al., 2015). The US Environmental Protection Agency considers all spent batteries as hazardous waste (Zand and Abduli, 2008). EU legislation (Directive 2006/66/EC) restrict that at least of 50% of the average weight of spent batteries should be recycled into materials for their primary application or for other applications besides energy recovery battery by 2011 (Dewulf et al., 2010). Similar legislation for spent LIBs of e-waste is either in force at different stages in Canada, China, South Africa, Mexico and so on.

[Eb94e25d779] section=3.6. Waste management of spent lithium-ion batteries
After collection of the spent LIBs, the recycling processes should be carried out to recover the valuable metals. The recycling method mainly include hydrometallurgy, pyrometallurgy and biometallurgy which have been used in industrial technologies throughout the world (Lv et al., 2018). In LithoRec process, which combines similar mechanical and hydrometallurgical methods, is very useful and effective for the mixed recovered spent LIBs (Kwade and Diekmann, 2018). In Umicore process, the spent batteries are smelted with flux to form a metal phase containing most of the Co. Ni and partial Fe to be refined and a slag phase containing Li and small amount of other metals to be further recovered (Gao et al., 2017; Marinos, 2014).

[E38896df1b0] section=3.6. Waste management of spent lithium-ion batteries
All in all, the management of spent LIBs need enhancing producer responsibility, increasing publicity, raising of public awareness, developing green public transport, and improving the inspection of the recycling companies (Bahaloo-Horeh and Mousavi, 2017; Sun et al., 2017). The researchers, governments, producers, recyclers and publics should have the responsibility for the management of spent LIBs.
