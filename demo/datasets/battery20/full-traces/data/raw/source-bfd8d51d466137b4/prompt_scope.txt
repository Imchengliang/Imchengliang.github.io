[E8b9f5ee5e3] section=1 Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries are being used increasingly due to their exceptional performance, which is one of the main reasons for the growth of lithium-ion batteries [1]. Lithium-ion battery vehicle sales reportedly exceed 10% of global vehicle sales. The market size for lithium batteries is expected to rise at a conforming compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 14.6% to reach approximately $92 billion in 2026 [2, 3]. Additionally, the massive use of lithium batteries will lead to an increasing number of spent lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). Large quantities of valuable metals, such as lithium, cobalt, nickel, and manganese, can be found in these spent LIBs. If these metals are not recycled, they will cause serious waste of resources and environmental pollution [4, 5].

[Ec6d14fa3a4] section=1 Introduction
Researchers have extensively researched extracting metals such as Li, Co, Ni, and Mn from spent LIBs. Pyrometallurgical and hydrometallurgical processes are the most established and commonly used processes [6]. Traditional pyrometallurgy involves roasting spent lithium batteries at high temperatures under a reducing atmosphere, similar to natural ores. Typical reducing agents include carbon [7 − 9] and some highly reducing active metals, such as aluminum and iron [10, 11]. Also, reducing gases, such as the hydrogen reduction method [12] or the methane reduction method [13], can be used. The convenience and high efficiency of pyrometallurgy have made it widely used. Hydrometallurgical techniques are favored over pyrometallurgy for several factors. Pyrometallurgy requires high energy consumption, high equipment requirements, emits harmful gases, and recovers high levels of metal impurities. On the other hand, hydrometallurgy has high recovery efficiency, low energy consumption, high product purity, and low levels of harmful gases [14, 15].

[Ef26cc0b603] section=1 Introduction
Hydrometallurgy is usually divided into acid leaching [15], alkaline leaching [16], and microbial leaching [17, 18]. There are two types of acids, organic and inorganic acids. Typically, inorganic acids are H SO [19], HCl [20, 21], HNO [22], and so forth. Inorganic acids are highly efficient in leaching, but they produce harmful by-products. In contrast, organic acids, including citric [23, 24], ascorbic [25], malonic acids [26], D, L-malic acid [27], and oxalic acid [28, 29], are more friendly to the environment. Several reducing agents are often used in the leaching process to disrupt the solid crystal structure of highly valent metals and to make the liquid-solid phase reaction more complete [30]. Reducing agents typically include, but are not limited to, hydrogen peroxide [31], glucose [32], and sodium sulfite [16]. However, this aforementioned indiscriminate leaching behavior for metals can lead to an overly complex composition of metal ions in an acid solution, which causes difficulties in separating valuable metals in the subsequent solution and potentially hazardous emissions of heavy metals, weakening its application prospects [33].

[Ec05b5801f4] section=1 Introduction
In recent years, selective leaching processes were found to be more suitable for recovering spent LIBs powder using the formation of a complex in leaching [34−37]. WANG et al [33] investigated the selective leaching of 96.4% nickel and 96.3% cobalt from spent lithium batteries using ammoniaammonium bicarbonate solution. MA et al [38] investigated that 97.7% nickel and 99.1% cobalt could be selectively leached from spent lithium batteries using ammonia and ammonium sulfite.

[Ef5a7d865ff] section=1 Introduction
However, these methods still suffer from difficulties, such as the need for reductive roasting and the additional wastewater discharge of sulfate from sulfites. In addition, the volatilization of ammonia makes the operating environment intensely harsh.

[Eb87874f3fc] section=1 Introduction
Therefore, based on the selectivity of the alkaline glycinate system for calcium, magnesium, and iron [39−41], this system has been proposed for the first time to leach spent LIBs to prepare relatively pure solutions containing Li, Co, Ni, and Mn, which facilitates the direct preparation of a new ternary precursor from it in the later stage [42]. The results show that the glycinate aqueous solution system can simultaneously leach Li, Co, Ni, and Mn in spent LIBs. The rest of the impurity metals, such as Al, Fe, and Cu, are hardly leached out. This also indicates that the glycinate aqueous solution system has a better selectivity for the valuable metals in the spent LIBs powder. In parallel, appropriate regulation rules for the leaching process are determined by analyzing the kinetics of the whole process and then studying its reaction mechanism for use in industrial production later.

[Ed0957d1ec4] section=2.2 Experimental method
As required by the experiment, a glycine solution at a specific concentration was made. The solution was warmed up in a water bath with a constant temperature while being stirred with a magnetic stirrer. Once the desired temperature was reached, solid NaOH pellets were added to the solution until the experimentally set pH value was achieved, which was monitored using a pH meter. After the solution reached the required values and stabilized at the specified temperature, 20 g of spent LIBs powder was added, and the reaction was timed. After the reaction time, the beaker was removed and left to cool to room temperature. Then, the leachate and leach residue were separated by vacuum filtration, and the metal content of the leach solution was measured. It was worth mentioning that experiments with reaction temperatures exceeding 100 °C needed to be carried out in a highpressure reactor. Due to the mixing of leaching and washing solutions, all concentrations of impurity metals in this paper were converted to concentrations in 100 mL of solution.

[Ec3c7b3120e] section=3.1 Reduction leaching
The leaching mechanism of the spent LIBs in the alkaline glycinate system is a process of reduction and complexation. The leaching mechanism is shown in Figure 3. The reaction between solid and glycine is a heterogeneous reaction, mainly occurring at the interface layer between the solid (metal oxide lattice) and liquid (alkaline glycine hydrogen peroxide solution) phases, and the reaction deepens over time. Under the reduction of hydrogen peroxide, Co, Ni, and Mn ions enter the solution as divalent metal ions and then complex with glycine. Li+ can dissolve directly in the system, while copper and aluminum exist in elemental form and cannot dissolve in the system.
