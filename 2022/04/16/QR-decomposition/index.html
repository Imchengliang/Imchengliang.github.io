The Least Squares Problem

The Least Squares Problem is one of the most important problem in numerical approximation. The main idea of the least squares problem is to solve the unknown parameters , so that the sum of the squares of the difference between the predicted value and the observed value (i.e. the error, or residual) is minimized. In short, it can be interpreted as solving the equation of Ax=bAx=bAx=b. In linear algebra, there are three common ways to solve the least squares problem, and their introduction and comparison are shown as below.

The normal equations

For an overdetermined systems (m∗nm*nm∗n): Ax=bAx=bAx=b such as:

The normal equations is ATAx^=ATbA^{T}A\hat{x}=A^{T}bATAx^=ATb. It’s a least squares question and normally requires ATAA^{T}AATA to be non-singular. It can prove that rank(A)=rank(ATA)rank(A)=rank(A^{T}A)rank(A)=rank(ATA), so AAA must have independent columns. The normal equations can be solved with Cholesky decomposition (ATAA^{T}AATA symm. pos. def.) but ATAA^{T}AATA is often ill-conditioned.

The normal equations requires less operation than other methods, but it won’t work when AAA is singular it also has a high condition number.

QR decomposition

QR decomposition A=QRA=QRA=QR is one way to solve least squares problem. The solution is shown as below.

ATAx=ATb⇒(QR)TQRx=(QR)Tb⇒RTQTQRx=RTQTb⇒RTRx=RTQTb⇒Rx=QTb\begin{gathered} A^{T} A x=A^{T} b \Rightarrow(Q R)^{T} Q R x=(Q R)^{T} b \Rightarrow \\ R^{T} Q^{T} Q R x=R^{T} Q^{T} b \Rightarrow R^{T} R x=R^{T} Q^{T} b \Rightarrow \\ R x=Q^{T} b \end{gathered} ATAx=ATb⇒(QR)TQRx=(QR)Tb⇒RTQTQRx=RTQTb⇒RTRx=RTQTb⇒Rx=QTb​

Solving Rx=QTbR x=Q^{T} bRx=QTb (with backward substitution) gives the least squares solution.

The merit of QR decomposition is that it has nothing to do with condition number. But the operation of QR decomposition is expensive and matrix AAA has to be singular otherwise there will be no solution for it.
